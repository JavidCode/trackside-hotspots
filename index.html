🦺 Rail Safety First

Trackside Hotspots exists to help railfans find great legal, public vantage points — not to encourage getting close to the tracks. Guidance below is based on Operation Lifesaver's official rail safety education.

The Basics

  • Stay at least 15 feet from the tracks at all times — trains overhang the rails and can strike you even if you're not standing on them.
  • Only cross tracks at designated crossings with a crossbuck, gate, or signal. Walking on or beside tracks anywhere else is illegal trespassing, not just unsafe.
  • Always expect a train — freight trains don't run on fixed schedules, and a train can approach from either direction on any track at any time.
  • It can take a train a mile or more to stop — roughly 18 football fields. By the time an engineer sees you, it's too late for the train to stop.

🔵 Know the Blue ENS Sign

Every public crossing has a small blue-and-white Emergency Notification System (ENS) sign, usually mounted on the crossing gate or signal mast. It lists two things:

  • A phone number that connects directly to that railroad's dispatch center — different for every railroad, not one universal number
  • A crossing ID number that tells dispatchers your exact location

Use it to report a stalled vehicle, a malfunctioning signal, a blocked crossing, or anything unsafe. In a true emergency, call 911 first — the ENS number is best for non-911 problems and for giving the railroad your exact crossing ID quickly.

📋 Railfan Safety Checklist

  • ☐ Parked legally, not blocking a road, driveway, or crossing
  • ☐ On public property, not private land or railroad right-of-way
  • ☐ At least 15 ft from the nearest rail, with solid footing
  • ☐ Aware of a possible second train on another track
  • ☐ Tripod, bag, and gear kept well clear of the ballast and rails
  • ☐ Not wearing headphones — you need to hear an approaching train
  • ☐ Told someone where you're headed, especially for remote spots

👀 If You See Something, Say Something

Railfans are often the first to notice a real problem — a person on the tracks, someone tampering with signals or equipment, a stalled vehicle, or suspicious activity near a bridge or crossing. Use the ENS sign or call 911 immediately. Reporting it isn't overreacting — it's exactly what these signs are there for.

When submitting a spot, please only share locations with legal public access and a safe viewing distance from the tracks — not private property or the right-of-way itself.

📚 Wiki & Links

Railfan Glossary

Consist — the specific set/order of locomotives and cars making up a train.

Subdivision — a named, defined segment of track a railroad operates as one unit (e.g. "CSX LCL Subdivision").

Dark territory — track with no signal system; train movements are governed entirely by radio authority instead of wayside signals.

CTC (Centralized Traffic Control) — a signal system where a dispatcher remotely controls signals and switches over a territory.

Defect detector — trackside equipment that scans passing trains for problems (overheating axles, dragging equipment, etc.) and radios an automated report.

Helper district — a stretch of steep grade where railroads add extra locomotives ("helpers") to assist trains, often a favorite spot for railfans.

Hump yard — a classification yard where cars are pushed over a small hill ("hump") and gravity-sorted onto different tracks.

DPU (Distributed Power Unit) — locomotive(s) placed mid-train or at the rear, controlled remotely by the lead engineer, instead of all power up front.

Crossbuck — the X-shaped "RAILROAD CROSSING" sign at a grade crossing; the standard, official name for that sign.

Restricted speed — a speed slow enough to stop within half the range of vision, short of a train, obstruction, or switch not properly lined.

EOT / FRED — End-Of-Train device, a small unit on the last car (railfans often call it a "FRED") that monitors brake-pipe pressure and can flash a rear light.

AAR channel — a standardized radio channel number (per the Association of American Railroads) used for railroad voice communication, e.g. "Channel 20."

Signals & Dispatching

Absolute signal — a signal that must be fully obeyed; a train facing "Stop" at an absolute signal must remain stopped until the dispatcher authorizes movement or the signal changes.

Permissive signal — a signal where a train facing "Stop" may, after coming to a full stop, proceed cautiously at restricted speed. Usually marked with a number plate or the letter "I" at its base.

Interlocking — a junction where tracks, signals, and switches are all controlled as one unit, so only non-conflicting train movements can be lined up at the same time.

ABS (Automatic Block Signal) — a system that divides track into sections called "blocks" and automatically sets signal aspects based on whether each block is occupied.

TWC (Track Warrant Control) — authorizing train movements by radio instead of (or alongside) wayside signals; the standard method used in dark territory.

Movement & Operations

Meet — when two trains headed in opposite directions on single track pass each other, usually with one holding in a siding while the other proceeds on the main.

Run-through power — locomotives from one railroad that continue running on another railroad's own rails under an operating agreement, instead of being swapped out at the interchange.

Foreign power — a locomotive operating on a railroad other than the one that owns it.

Doubling the hill — splitting a train into two halves and taking each up a steep grade separately, when it's too heavy to climb in one pull.

Local / way freight — a train that works a subdivision one industry at a time, setting out and picking up cars along the route, rather than running straight through.

Trains & Equipment

Manifest — a general freight train carrying a mixed variety of car types and commodities, as opposed to a unit train.

Unit train — a train made up entirely of one car type carrying a single commodity (coal, grain, ethanol, etc.) between one origin and one destination.

Intermodal — a train carrying shipping containers or truck trailers on well cars or flat cars, for combined rail/truck/ship transport.

Bad order — a rail car pulled from service due to a mechanical defect, marked and set aside until it's repaired.

Cut — a group of cars coupled together, smaller than a full train, being moved as one unit during switching.

Railfan Culture

Fallen flag — a railroad that no longer exists as an independent company, usually after a merger or acquisition (e.g. Penn Central, Southern Pacific, Conrail).

Heritage unit — a modern locomotive painted in the colors and scheme of a predecessor or fallen-flag railroad to honor its history.

Patched unit — a locomotive still wearing its previous owner's paint scheme, with only the reporting marks and number updated ("patched") rather than fully repainted.

Roll-by — when a crew member visually inspects a passing train from trackside (or from another train) and radios back any defects spotted.
